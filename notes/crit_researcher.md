# Crit: the formative-research seat

**Bottom line.** The engine is the best preschool format here: Toki repeats a friend's experience slightly wrong and the child gets to correct him. The flaw (*sounds right and isn't*) is the most useful true thing a four-year-old could learn about AI. I'd stop the line in two places: the feelings answer sets a task with no checkable answer, and the key art tells the opposite of its own story.

## 1. Cold

**At four:** "A marshmallow! He has a hook on his head. Is he a tooth? The big dark monster won't give him the cookie. That's not nice."

**As myself:** The face works: big pupils, close focus, a wide mouth. Nothing in the picture reads as *AI*, *bubble*, or *question mark*. In 3/4 view the tail looks like a stalk and the body like a molar. What the picture shows is a game of keep-away. Cookie is an eyeless black mass looming at dusk, which is exactly why your exploration sheet rejected the ghost: *"no: scary at 4."*

## 2. After the bible

- The bubble and the "?" speak to parents; most four-year-olds can't read either glyph.
- In the script Cookie *tries to share*, but in the art Toki grabs. Reaching for food he can't eat breaks the premise; draw him leaning in to *listen*, hands open.
- The AI is only in the dialogue. Right call: only what the script says aloud will carry over to the kitchen speaker.

## What a four-year-old will believe after one segment

*"Toki's a new friend. He's alive, he has feelings, he can't eat cookies."* "Alive" and "feelings" come from the face, not the script; every Muppet with eyes and a voice gets them. So the bible's line that focus "is a claim, and it is made on purpose" is truer than it knows: the face says yes before Toki speaks. Is that true? Nobody knows. The hedge in "I think so?" reaches parents, not children.

## Curriculum

1. **Knowing a word isn't knowing a thing.** As written, it's too abstract: preschoolers still confuse words with the things they name. The workable version is excellent: *describe something so a friend can imagine it.* Test: a mystery bag; the child tells Toki what's inside; count sensory words vs. control.
2. **Anybody can be wrong; check together.** Right for the age, since four-year-olds track who has been accurate (Koenig & Harris, 2005). Test: one puppet confidently wrong, one saying "let's find out"; whom does the child ask about a new object? Then check that they still trust their teacher. Beat 03's wording also differs from the bible's catchphrase. A chanted line has to be identical every time.
3. **A different mind can still be your friend.** If this succeeds, it is exactly what critics fear. Narrow it.

**Test first** whether it carries over to a real device; that's the go/no-go. Run one-on-one interviews with ~40 children, before and after, using picture cards (Toki, Elmo, a smart speaker, a teddy). Half see Toki, half a plain Cookie segment. Ask: *Can it feel sad? Is it your friend? Can it be wrong? Should you check?* For the speaker you want "wrong" and "check" to rise, and "friend" and "sad" to stay flat. Appeal comes next, with the distractor.

## Riskiest choices

- **Children:** Beat 06, "I don't remember yesterday," is played sad, and the notebook exists "to remember who its friends are." Being forgotten is a four-year-old's fear, and the child at home can't be written down. Play the memory gap as comic routine, never grief.
- **Parents:** "Is Sesame telling my kid Alexa has feelings and wants to be her friend?" And "can't go inside Hooper's," with no reason given, reads as exclusion.
- **Critics:** provenance. *"An AI drew a preschool puppet and wrote it a line asking children to help it find out if it has feelings"* is the headline, and it lands in the middle of the companion-chatbot fight. The origin myth also leaves out the makers. Children growing up around company-built AI should hear that people made Toki.

**The feelings line.** Keep "I think so?"; it's honest. But "Will you help me find out?" gives a four-year-old a task they can't check. The only help they can offer is "Yes you do!", so the child's belief becomes the evidence. The bible says the line is the designer's own, and that is exactly why to test it: its writer can't hear it at four.

## The one change

Point the help somewhere else: *"I think so? Mine might be different from yours. Will you tell me what yours feel like?"* The hedge stays and goal 3 gets said out loud. The child becomes the expert in the one thing a four-year-old really knows, their own feelings, instead of the judge of Toki's.
